A framework that protects the point where real data enters a synthetic driving dataset, then tests, measures and verifies that protection.

Literature Survey

Public driving datasets such as KITTI and the Waymo Open Dataset needed large recording and labeling efforts. Recordings of identifiable people are personal data under the GDPR.

Simulators such as CARLA generate labeled camera, depth and segmentation data. They are configured from real statistics, and their papers do not discuss protecting those statistics.

Differential privacy (Dwork et al.) limits how much one record can change an output. DP-SGD (Abadi et al.) applies it to deep learning, and Rényi DP (Mironov) tracks the budget over many training steps.

Membership inference attacks (Shokri et al.; Yeom et al.; Carlini et al.) show that trained models can leak their training data. Synthetic data without a formal mechanism does not reliably protect source records (Stadler et al.).

Research Problem

Autonomous vehicle simulators are configured from real driving statistics without privacy protection, models trained on their output have no formal guarantee, and privacy budgets are rarely tested against real attacks. Developers have no measurable and verifiable way to show that synthetic driving data was produced without exposing the people in the real data.

Research Objectives

Main objective

Integrate ε-differential privacy into a synthetic autonomous vehicle data pipeline, with a total budget of ε ≤ 10, a membership inference attack success rate of 55% or less, and perception accuracy within 15% of a real-data baseline.

Methodology

  1. 1 Generate
  2. 2 Protect
  3. 3 Measure
  4. 4 Verify

1Generate

CARLA renders RGB, depth, segmentation, class masks and YOLO labels across five towns from privatized calibration statistics. Every file is encrypted with AES-256 and listed in a SHA-256 manifest.

2Protect

The Laplace mechanism privatizes seven calibration statistics within a total budget of ε = 1.8. Opacus DP-SGD with an RDP accountant trains models under a hard budget (ε = 1, 3 and 8 tested, three seeds each), and a stress test on a deliberately overfitted model confirms the protection.

3Measure

YOLOv8 detection and FID compare synthetic data with real KITTI images. A poisoning audit removes bad labels before training.

4Verify

Signed HMAC-SHA256 audit reports and a privacy certificate are checked independently, including a composition check on the privacy budget.

Key Findings

56.7%→ to 50.3%

Membership inference attack success without and with differential privacy (50.3% to 51.6% across ε = 1, 3 and 8). 50% is random guessing, so the attack fell to chance.

0.628→ to 0.501

Stress test over five seeds: attack AUC on a deliberately overfitted model, without differential privacy and at ε = 1. The drop was significant at all three budgets (p ≤ 0.001), and stronger privacy gave a weaker attack.

7.9%→ to 1.1%

A stronger, reference-calibrated attack (after LiRA) caught 7.9% of training frames at a 1% false-positive rate without differential privacy, and 1.1% at ε = 1, which is chance. Three attacks in total confirm the result.

0.16 to 0.21

The privacy cost: the drop in test AUC when models were trained with differential privacy.

10.46%

A detector trained on the synthetic data reached 10.46% of the real-data mAP50 on KITTI images, so the 15% utility target was not reached with the current synthetic data.

ε = 1.8

The calibration now spends ε = 1.8 in total across seven queries (0.257 each). The independent composition check found that the earlier setup totalled ε = 12.6; the corrected version passes the same check.
